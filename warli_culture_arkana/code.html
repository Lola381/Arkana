Warli

Maharashtra · 2500 BCE – Present

About

Warli painting is a form of tribal art mostly created by the tribal people from the North Sahyadri Range in Maharashtra, India. This traditional art form, characterized by its rudimentary graphic vocabulary, uses a circle, a triangle, and a square to represent the world. The circle and triangle come from their observation of nature, the circle representing the sun and the moon, the triangle derived from mountains and pointed trees.

Unlike other Indian folk art forms, Warli paintings do not depict mythological characters or images of deities. Instead, they portray the social life of the tribal people. Scenes of human figures engaged in hunting, dancing, sowing, and harvesting are common, reflecting a profound harmony with nature. The pristine white pigment, traditionally made from a paste of rice flour and water, stands in stark, beautiful contrast to the austere mud and cow dung backgrounds.

Timeline

2500 BCE - 3000 BCE

Origins in Antiquity

Rooted in ancient traditions, early forms of Warli art are believed to have originated in the Sahyadri ranges. The geometric motifs parallel prehistoric cave paintings.

10th Century CE

Establishment of Ritual Practice

The art form solidifies as a vital component of marriage ceremonies and harvest festivals, painted exclusively by the women of the tribe (Savasinis).

1970s

Commercial Introduction

Jivya Soma Mashe, an artist from the Warli tribe, diverges from tradition by painting on a daily basis for artistic expression rather than solely for rituals, bringing global recognition.

Present Day

Global Heritage

Warli motifs adorn modern fashion, interiors, and digital spaces. Efforts continue to preserve the authentic techniques and intellectual property of the indigenous creators.
